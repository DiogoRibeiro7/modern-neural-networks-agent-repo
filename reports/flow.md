## 3. Method

Full detail is in
[`src/modern_nn_lab/tracks/flow/README.md`](../src/modern_nn_lab/tracks/flow/README.md).

Generation as vector-field regression. Both probability paths are affine in the endpoints,
`x_t = α_t·x₁ + σ_t·x₀`, so the conditional velocity is exactly `α̇_t·x₁ + σ̇_t·x₀` with no
approximation anywhere — which is why the tests can assert the target field exactly rather
than within a tolerance.

**Read the `field RMSE` column first: it does not move.** It is identical at every step
count, because it is computed pointwise with no solver involved. That is not a coincidence
to be noted in passing — it is the check that the separation is real. A quantity that
drifted with step count would mean the two errors were still entangled.

**One caution about how the metric scales.** Energy distance is roughly *quadratic* in a
small displacement error, so a first-order solver shows second-order-looking decay in this
table: Euler's error falls by about 4× per doubling of steps, not 2×. The solver's actual
convergence orders are 1 and 2, measured directly against an exact ODE solution in
[`tests/test_flow.py`](../tests/test_flow.py). Reading orders off this table would be a
mistake.

## 7. Interpretation

**The two error sources are cleanly separated, and they dominate in different regimes.**
Discretization error falls with step count until it reaches the sampling-noise floor
(~0.0015–0.0016, against a floor of 0.0018). Approximation error does not move at all — the
field RMSE is a constant 0.1368 (linear) and 0.1602 (trigonometric), about 4.6% and 5.0% of
the field's own magnitude. The combined curve tracks the discretization curve while steps
are few, then flattens at roughly 0.0037–0.0042 rather than at the floor. That residual gap
is what the network's error costs, and it is invisible in any single sample-quality number.

The practical reading: below about 16 Euler steps this model is **solver-limited**, and
above it, **model-limited**. Spending more steps past that point buys nothing, and the only
way forward is a better field.

**The optimal-transport-style path is markedly easier to integrate.** At two Euler steps the
linear path scores 0.1084 against the trigonometric path's 0.6261 — nearly 6× worse — and
the trigonometric path needs roughly 8–16× more Euler steps to reach the same error. This is
the expected consequence of the geometry rather than a surprise: the linear path's
conditional trajectories are straight lines traversed at constant speed, which a first-order
method integrates almost exactly, while the trigonometric path's are quarter-circles with
varying speed, which it cannot. The required ablation therefore has a clear winner *at low
step counts*, and essentially no winner once the solver is adequate.

**Second-order integration erases most of that difference.** With midpoint, both paths are
at the floor by 2–4 steps. The path choice matters most precisely where the compute budget
is tightest, which is also where it is most often cited as mattering.

**A caveat on the numbers near the floor.** Several midpoint entries sit at or slightly below
0.0018 — the trigonometric path reads 0.0010 at four steps. Those are indistinguishable from
a perfect match: the energy-distance estimator has its own sampling noise, and a value below
the floor means the two sample sets differ by less than two draws from the target differ
from each other. No ranking should be read among entries in that band.

## 8. Limitations

- **Two dimensions only**, deliberately, per the prompt. Nothing here transfers to
  high-dimensional generation, where the interesting problems are different in kind and not
  merely in scale.
- **The error separation exists only for the Gaussian target.** `moons` and `mixture` have no
  closed-form marginal field, so §5 does not cover them and their §4 numbers confound the
  two error sources exactly as this track argues against.
- **No adaptive-step solver.** Both methods use a fixed step size, so the step-count axis is
  not what a practical sampler would use.
- **The paths differ in more than one way.** Linear versus trigonometric changes both the
  trajectory shape and the speed profile. §7 attributes the result to that combination and
  does not separate them; doing so would need a path with straight trajectories at
  non-uniform speed.
- **No minibatch optimal-transport coupling.** The linear path is optimal-transport-*style*
  in that each conditional trajectory is a straight line, but the pairing of source and
  target samples is independent, not an optimal coupling. That is a real distinction and the
  name should not be read as claiming the stronger property.
- **One network size and one training budget**, not swept. The 4.6% relative field error is a
  property of this configuration, not a floor for the method.

## 9. Next experiments

1. Sweep the training budget and re-run §5. The crossover between solver-limited and
   model-limited is the interesting quantity, and it should move with field quality — a
   prediction this track states but does not test.
2. Add minibatch optimal-transport coupling and compare against the independent pairing used
   here. That is the ablation that would justify the "optimal transport" name.
3. Add a path with straight trajectories but non-uniform speed, to separate the two
   explanations §7 currently offers together.
4. Extend the closed-form reference to a Gaussian *mixture* target, where the marginal field
   is still tractable, so the error separation covers a multi-modal case rather than only a
   unimodal one.
