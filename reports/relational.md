## 1. Claim level

**`research prototype`** — see [`docs/claim_policy.md`](../docs/claim_policy.md), where this
is the weakest level, and the correct one here.

A compact model that learns over linked tables without flattening them first. What is
**not** claimed: any reproduction of a relational foundation model, any statement about one,
or any result at a scale where the word "foundation" would be earned. No pre-trained
relational checkpoint is invoked anywhere in this track; the prompt's optional KumoRFM-2
adapter is **not** implemented, so nothing here can be mistaken for its reproduction.

## 3. Method

Full detail is in
[`src/modern_nn_lab/tracks/relational/README.md`](../src/modern_nn_lab/tracks/relational/README.md).

A row stays a row. Each prediction point `(entity, t*)` becomes a set of typed, linked rows —
the entity, its recent visible events, the rows those events point at, and rows from a
distractor table — each carrying its table type, elapsed time, columns, and the slot of the
row it references. That last channel is the foreign key, and it is what makes a two-hop path
distinguishable from two unrelated rows in the same bag.

**Two rounds of message passing is the minimum that can reach a linked row's attributes.**
That is asserted rather than argued: a one-round model's prediction does not move when a
product's price changes, and a two-round model's does
([`tests/test_relational.py`](../tests/test_relational.py)).

**What is held equal.** Every model — including the GBDT's engineered features — receives
its view of the database from the same sampler, so no comparison here differs in what it was
allowed to see. Widths are searched per variant to match the prototype's parameter count,
because removing the typed encoders deletes three quarters of them.

## 4. Temporal leakage

The rule: a row is visible to prediction `(e, t*)` if it is static, or if `t_row < t*`. The
inequality is strict — a row timestamped at the prediction instant is not visible, and
admitting simultaneous events is an easily-missed leak. There is a test for exactly that.

It is enforced in one module and nowhere else. Four things are gated on it:

1. **features** — only visible rows are encoded;
2. **neighbourhoods** — an invisible event does not reveal the row it points at;
3. **normalization** — statistics fitted on training rows only;
4. **the split** — chronological, so no model trains on a period it is later scored on.

A fifth is handled in the generator: labels threshold a statistic at zero rather than at a
population quantile, so target construction never consults entities whose prediction times
lie in the future.

### The canary makes the tests non-vacuous

Asserting "we do not leak" proves nothing if there is nothing to find. Every database
contains post-timestamp orders whose amount encodes the label exactly — legitimate rows that
simply have not happened yet. The audit reports what a leaking pipeline would score, beside
what the sampler actually sees:

## 8. Interpretation

**The prototype does not beat its baselines, and on three of five regimes it loses.** On
`one_hop`, `multi_hop` and `irrelevant`, the flattened GBDT is ahead by 0.03-0.08, and the
homogeneous `gnn-flat` — which discards foreign keys entirely and mean-pools every row into
the entity — matches or beats the full model on four of the five. Explicit foreign-key
message passing did not pay for itself here. That is the headline, and no reading of the
table supports a softer one.

**One regime is a decisive exception, and it is the one about time.** On `temporal` the
prototype scores 0.836 against 0.678 for `gnn-flat` and 0.644 for the GBDT — the only place
anything beats the hand-written joins. The ablation confirms the mechanism rather than
leaving it to inference: removing the elapsed-time channel and its gate drops `temporal`
from 0.836 to **0.547**, a fall of 0.289, which is by far the largest single-flag effect
anywhere in §7 and is confined to the regime that requires it. Elsewhere `no-time` costs
nothing. So time is load-bearing, exactly where the design predicted and nowhere else.

**Typing is not load-bearing at all.** `no-types` is within seed noise in every regime, and
on `multi_hop` it is *better* than the full model (0.893 against 0.878). One shared row
projection is sufficient for a four-table schema; the per-table encoders are capacity spent
without return.

**Why the structure bought so little, which is the most useful thing this track learned.**
The sampler has already done the join. A product row appears in a neighbourhood *only
because* a visible order pointed at it, so the relational structure is largely encoded in
*which rows are present* before any model sees them. Given that, mean-pooling reaches a
product's attributes in one step, while the message-passing model needs two rounds and
dilutes them through an intermediate. On a two-hop star schema, a bag of the right rows is
close to sufficient.

**That means the comparison this track actually ran is narrower than the one it set out to
run.** Neither "flat" baseline is flat in the sense the relational argument is about: both
consume relationally-sampled, time-gated neighbourhoods. What §6 measures is whether
explicit foreign-key propagation beats pooling over a correctly-joined bag of rows — and the
answer is that it does not, except when time matters. It does *not* measure whether
relational modelling beats flattening, because the flattening here was handed the join. §10
says how to fix that, and it is the first thing to fix.

**The control behaved.** On `cold_start` the target-only floor scores 0.989, the highest in
its row, and every relational model sits at or just below it. There is no history to read
there, so that is the correct outcome and a model beating it would have indicated a leak
rather than skill.

## 9. Limitations

- **The database is synthetic and small.** Four tables, one link depth of two, 600 entities,
  and a schema this repository wrote. Nothing here transfers to a real warehouse without
  evidence that is not collected.
- **Seed variance covers training only.** The database is generated once per regime from a
  fixed seed, so the intervals describe initialization and optimization noise, not
  variability across databases. They are narrower than they look.
- **The GBDT baseline's joins were written by hand**, with knowledge of what each regime
  depends on. That makes it a strong baseline and a fair one for these five regimes, but it
  flatters the flattening approach relative to a setting where nobody knows in advance which
  aggregate matters — which is the setting the relational argument is actually about.
- **Neither "flat" baseline is actually flat.** Both consume the sampler's relationally
  joined, time-gated neighbourhoods, so §6 compares foreign-key propagation against pooling
  over a correctly-joined bag — not relational modelling against flattening. This is the
  track's largest design limitation and it materially narrows what §6 can be cited for.
  See §8 and next experiment 2.
- **Neighbourhoods are truncated** at 8 events and 6 distractors, most-recent-first. An
  entity with more history is not fully represented for any model, including the baselines.
- **One hop depth, one link shape.** No self-referential links, no many-to-many, no text
  columns, and the two-hop path is the deepest thing tested. §8's explanation for why
  message passing added little is specific to a shallow star schema and may not survive a
  deeper one — which is a prediction, not a result.
- **`gbdt-flat` records a parameter count of zero**, because it is not a Torch model and the
  contract's counter does not apply to a tree ensemble. It is not a zero-capacity model, and
  the parameter-matching in §7 covers the neural models only.
- **No reference model.** The optional relational foundation-model adapter is not
  implemented, so nothing here bears on how a real one behaves.

## 10. Next experiments

1. **Give the baselines a genuinely flat view** — target-table columns plus aggregates over
   an *unjoined* dump of each related table — and re-run §6. This is the comparison the
   track set out to make and did not make (§8), and until it is run, no claim about
   relational modelling versus flattening should be drawn from this track.
2. Deepen the schema to three hops and re-run §7 with `n_rounds` swept. §8 argues that
   message passing added little because a shallow star schema barely needs it; a deeper
   schema is the direct test of that explanation, and the one-round test shows the
   architecture's depth is at least mechanically load-bearing.
3. Give the GBDT *only* generic aggregates, with no regime-specific joins, and report both
   numbers. The gap is the honest measure of what schema knowledge is worth.
4. Drop the per-table encoders. `no-types` matched or beat the full model everywhere, so the
   simpler architecture is the better default until some schema shows otherwise.
5. Vary the database seed as well as the training seed, so intervals cover the thing they
   appear to cover.
6. Add a leakage regime where the canary is _subtle_ rather than perfect — a shortcut worth
   0.6 accuracy rather than 1.0 — since a perfect canary is the easiest possible thing to
   detect.
