## 3. Method

Full detail is in
[`src/modern_nn_lab/tracks/moe/README.md`](../src/modern_nn_lab/tracks/moe/README.md).

Three layers sit inside identical scaffolding — the same projection, residual, and readout —
so a difference in the task metric is attributable to the layer:

- **`dense-ffn`** — one feed-forward network, widened to match the expert bank's parameter
  count. The conventional baseline whose cost sparsity is trying to beat.
- **`dense-moe`** — every expert on every token, combined by the full gate. Milestone 1 of
  the prompt, and the *reference* a sparse layer approximates: the gap between them is the
  price of sparsity, measured rather than argued about.
- **`sparse-moe`** — top-k dispatch with capacity.

**The task makes specialization measurable rather than inferable.** Every token is generated
by one of four known functions and that identity is recorded. Each token splits into a
*selector* half that determines which function applies and an independent *value* half the
function acts on. Without that split, a model could partly regress the target without
routing at all, and a failure to specialize would be indistinguishable from a task that
never needed it. A test confirms the property: logistic regression recovers the latent
function from the selector half at >0.85 and from the value half at <0.45.

## 7. Interpretation

**Sparsity was nearly free at top-2.** `sparse-top2` reaches 0.0312 against the dense
ensemble's 0.0301 — a difference well inside the seed spread of either — while activating
53% of the parameters and spending half the FLOPs. It is also the only variant that
genuinely specialized: purity 0.874, normalized entropy 0.200, and a confusion matrix with
three clean diagonal entries. This is the track's positive result, and it is the shape the
mixture-of-experts claim predicts.

**The most useful thing here is a bug in this repository's own router.** `sparse-top1`
scored 0.1395 — worse than a plain dense feed-forward network — at a normalized routing
entropy of **0.999**, meaning the router never committed to anything. The cause is
arithmetic, not optimization: with one expert kept and gates renormalized to sum to one, the
kept gate is `g / g ≡ 1`, a constant in the router's parameters, so **the task loss
contributes exactly zero gradient to routing**. Measured directly, the router's gradient
norm is 6.7 × 10⁻⁹ against 3.5 × 10⁻² without renormalization. The auxiliary balancing loss
was then the only signal shaping the router, and it pushes towards *uniform* — precisely the
opposite of specialization, which is why the entropy sat at its ceiling.

**Removing the renormalization restores it, and the improvement is large.**
`sparse-top1-raw` is identical in every respect except that flag: same parameters, same
FLOPs, same expert bank. Error falls from 0.1395 to **0.0847**, a 39% reduction; entropy
falls from 0.999 to 0.448 as the router starts committing; purity rises from 0.427 to 0.607.
A one-line change, free in compute, recovering most of the gap.

Two honest qualifications on that. Its seed spread is wide (±0.0371), so its overlap with
`dense-ffn` at 0.0898 ± 0.0058 means "comparable", not "better". And it still does not reach
`sparse-top2`: committing to one expert also makes routing lumpy enough to drop 9% of
tokens. Top-2 remains the better operating point here.

**Uniform utilization can be a symptom rather than a sign of health.** `sparse-capacity0.5`
shows utilization of exactly 0.250 across all four experts — the most balanced row in §5 —
while being the *worst* model in the table at 0.2731. The balance is an artefact of the
capacity ceiling binding on every expert, not of good routing, and the entropy of 0.999
beside it says the router is uninformative. Read alone, that utilization column would have
been actively misleading.

**The balancing loss does what it claims.** Removing it (`sparse-aux0`) widens utilization
from [0.186, 0.308] to [0.139, 0.433] and pushes dropped tokens from 0% to 27.8%, with error
rising to 0.1551.

**The diagnostics earned their place in the acceptance criteria.** On accuracy alone the
conclusion would have been "top-1 sparsity hurts quality" — plausible, publishable, and
wrong. The entropy column named the cause, and the fix followed from the cause rather than
from a hyperparameter search.

## 8. Limitations

- **The task is synthetic and small.** Four functions, four experts, 32-wide model, one
  mixture layer. Nothing here speaks to the scale at which mixture-of-experts models are
  actually used, where the routing problem is harder and the hardware story is different.
- **Throughput does not generalize.** Sparse dispatch here is a gather, several small
  matmuls, and a scatter in Python. A fused kernel with expert parallelism — the only
  setting where sparsity pays off in practice — is not implemented and not measured. The
  measured numbers describe this implementation on this machine and nothing else.
- **Seed variance covers training only.** One dataset is generated from a fixed seed and
  shared by every model, so the intervals describe initialization and optimization noise,
  not variability across task draws.
- **Experts equal functions.** With four experts and four generating functions, the routing
  problem is exactly solvable. Nothing here shows what happens when experts are scarcer than
  the structure, or far more plentiful.
- **One capacity and one auxiliary weight below the default** were tested, not a sweep. The
  overflow and balancing results show direction, not a response curve.
- **Purity has a known blind spot.** It measures whether each function has a home, not
  whether each expert has one function, so two functions sharing an expert still score well.
  It must be read beside the utilization spread, which is why both are in §5 and §6.

## 9. Next experiments

1. Sweep the number of experts against the number of generating functions, in both
   directions. The equal case tested here is the easiest one.
2. Add expert-choice routing, where experts select tokens rather than the reverse, which
   removes the capacity-drop failure mode by construction rather than by tuning.
3. Sweep capacity factor and auxiliary weight properly, and report the quality/drop
   trade-off as a curve.
4. Measure throughput with a batched-expert implementation to separate "sparsity is slow" —
   which is false at scale — from "this dispatch loop is slow", which is what §4 actually
   shows.
